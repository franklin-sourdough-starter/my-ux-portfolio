Evaluating & Redesigning the Usability of a Gamified EdTech Solution

Heuristic evaluation and usability research on a math-learning platform for middle schoolers, uncovering the information-hierarchy problems that were quietly working against its own gamification.

Role
UX Research Consultant
Organization
dfusion Inc.
Duration
October 2024 – January 2025 (8 weeks)
Methods
Heuristic evaluation, usability testing, semi-structured interviews, competitor research
Context

Gamified, but not necessarily usable

dfusion's EdTech product gamifies math learning for middle school students — game mechanics layered onto core academic content, aimed at keeping kids engaged with material they might otherwise disengage from. But gamification can't do its job if students can't tell what's happening on screen. My engagement started from a simple question: were the game mechanics actually working with the learning experience, or quietly working against it?

My Role & Approach

Audit first, then talk to real users

I ran a structured heuristic evaluation across the core dashboards and student-facing interfaces, using established usability and human-factors principles to flag violations systematically rather than relying on gut reactions. I paired that audit with semi-structured interviews and usability testing sessions to check whether the issues I flagged actually caused the confusion I predicted — and to catch what a heuristic pass alone would miss.

In parallel, I ran competitor research across supplementary educational curricula, and reached out directly to local public school districts to understand classroom needs from the educator's side, not just the student's.

Key Findings

Three violations, one root cause

  • Misleading information hierarchy — game elements (points, streaks, rewards) frequently competed visually with the academic task itself, so students' attention landed on the wrong layer of the screen.
  • Limited visibility of system status — students often couldn't tell whether an action had registered, whether progress had saved, or what state the game/lesson was actually in.
  • High cognitive load — for the target age group, the interface asked students to hold too much simultaneously in working memory just to navigate, before they'd even engaged with the math content.

Individually these read as standard heuristic violations. Together, interviews confirmed they compounded into something bigger: students were spending real cognitive effort decoding the interface itself, effort that should have gone toward the math.

Design Response

Wireframes built around what students actually noticed first

Based on the findings, I designed wireframes, mockups, and interactive prototypes for the core dashboards and student-facing interfaces — restructuring the visual hierarchy so system status and academic task state were unambiguous before any gamified layer competed for attention, and simplifying navigation to reduce the load on a middle-school working-memory span.

Impact

Reflection

This project was where I first felt design's making process fully in my own hands, not just informed by my research. Anthropology taught me to ask why a 12-year-old glances at the wrong part of a screen; design gave me the tools to actually move that glance somewhere useful.
