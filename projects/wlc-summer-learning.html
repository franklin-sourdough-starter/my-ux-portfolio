Exploring Summer in WLC Schools: A Landscape Analysis

A 7-week, mixed-methods study of 18 summer learning programs across three Delaware school districts — built to give a $1.5M funding decision a ground-level view it didn't have.

Role
Strategic Support Fellow
Organization
Wilmington Learning Collaborative
Duration
June – August 2025 (7 weeks)
Methods
Program observation, semi-structured interviews, survey, thematic coding (MAXQDA)
Context

$1.5M in funding, and no picture of the ground

The Wilmington Learning Collaborative had allocated $1.5M across summer learning programs in the Christina, Brandywine, and Red Clay school districts — nine partnering schools, Pre-K through 8th grade. What existed was a funding breakdown and a general sense of what was happening. What was missing was the day-to-day reality: what these programs actually looked like on the ground, and whether the people running and attending them experienced them the way funders assumed.

My research question, at its core, was one I recognized from anthropology: numbers alone don't tell you what a program feels like to live inside. The Collaborative needed a landscape analysis grounded in the perspectives of the people actually running and attending these programs — instructors, coordinators, district leaders, and families.

Approach

Field visits over a wider, shallower scope

Given the 7-week window, I made a deliberate scope trade-off: rather than stretch thin across every possible summer opportunity, I focused the study on the 18 programs actually happening at the 9 WLC schools, run either directly by the districts or in partnership with community organizations — and stayed attentive to family perspectives throughout.

  • Program level — 15 program observations (3–6 hours each) for rich, contextual, ethnographic detail, plus semi-structured interviews with instructors and coordinators, often carried out as casual conversation between activities so as not to disrupt operations.
  • Family level — A 13-question survey distributed physically and electronically through schools and districts, reaching families whose children were enrolled and those who opted out — 180 responses collected via SurveyMonkey.
  • District level — 6 semi-structured phone or video interviews with program directors, district leadership, and summer learning coordinators.

I organized field notes and interview transcripts by topic and coded them thematically in MAXQDA, then analyzed the closed- and open-ended survey data alongside the qualitative themes.

Key Findings

18 programs, one shared strategy, four categories of friction

18
summer programs mapped across 7 active WLC schools
57%
average attendance rate uncovered across programs
4
categories of pain points identified in the planning timeline

All 18 programs centered on literacy and math, averaging 5 weeks in length (early July to early August), and all were offered to families at no cost. But program-level strategy masked significant variation underneath — in staffing consistency, planning lead time, and how well district-level decisions reached the instructors actually running each site day to day.

The 57% average attendance figure, combined with instructor and coordinator interviews, pointed toward planning-timeline friction as a root cause worth surfacing on its own — not just a symptom to note in passing.

Limitations

What I chose not to claim

I was direct with the Collaborative about the study's edges, the same way I was trained to be about ethnographic claims: this was an exploratory, single-summer landscape analysis, not a curriculum evaluation. I didn't have instructional-hour data or pre/post test scores to measure academic effectiveness, and short data-collection windows meant some district-level systems of support were represented by only one or two available staff. I framed the report as a catalyst for future research, not a final verdict.

Outcome & Impact

From a report to a funding strategy

I synthesized the findings into evaluation timelines, benchmarks, and KPIs meant to guide how the Collaborative measures academic ROI on summer learning going forward — turning a one-summer landscape analysis into a repeatable measurement structure for the $1.5M investment. I presented the recommendations directly to the mayor, state senators, and education leaders across school districts in Delaware and Pennsylvania; the findings were later featured on a statewide news channel.

Reflection

This was the first project where I felt the full arc of my path click into place. The observation hours were pure anthropology — sitting in a gym or a classroom for six hours just to understand a program's rhythm. But turning 180 survey responses, 15 site visits, and 6 leadership interviews into a KPI framework a mayor's office could actually act on — that was the design instinct, the one that refuses to stop at "here's what we found."
