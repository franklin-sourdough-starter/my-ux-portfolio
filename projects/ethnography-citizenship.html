Academic · Ethnography

Ethnography — Cybersecurity, Citizenship & Cultural Identity of International Students Abroad

The research that convinced me insight could move institutions: 20 interviews into how international students self-censor online to protect a life half a world away.

Role
Lead Researcher (Honors Thesis)
Institution
New York University, Dept. of Anthropology
Duration
Fall 2022
Methods
Semi-structured qualitative interviews, thematic analysis
Context

Living under two sets of rules at once

My final undergraduate year explored the relationship between learning, technology, and higher education for a specific, under-examined group: Chinese international students navigating U.S. campus life while remaining digitally, legally, and socially tethered to home. I wanted to understand how they used digital technology day to day — and what that use revealed about the pressures they were actually living under, not just the ones a survey would ask about directly.

Approach

Qualitative interviews, held long enough to get past the rehearsed answer

I conducted qualitative interviews with 20 international students to explore how they experienced media surveillance and navigated questions of identity while abroad. This wasn't a quick-turnaround survey study — I was after the moment in a conversation where a participant stopped giving the answer they thought was expected and started describing what they actually did, and why.

I analyzed the interview data to identify recurring themes in digital privacy behavior and cultural adaptation, coding across transcripts to see where individual coping strategies converged into a shared, patterned practice.

Key Finding

"Ultra-Flexible Citizenship"

The throughline across nearly every interview was a form of citizenship I came to describe as Ultra-Flexible: students living, in effect, under two overlapping sets of civic and digital norms simultaneously. To protect their lives, families, and futures back home, they engaged in constant, largely invisible self-censorship online — carefully managing what they posted, searched, and said, even while physically living in a country with very different expectations around speech and privacy.

"Living under this unique form of citizenship, students need to constantly self-censor digitally to avoid potential consequences that could affect their lives."

This wasn't framed by participants as an occasional inconvenience — it was a constant, low-grade cognitive and emotional labor, woven into ordinary digital life: choosing an app, phrasing a caption, deciding whether to join a class discussion thread.

Outcome & Impact

From findings to institutional recommendations

I translated the findings into recommendations delivered directly to professors and college leadership, aimed at making digital safety and cybersecurity guidance more culturally sensitive to international students' actual constraints — and at strengthening these students' sense of belonging on campus rather than treating their caution as disengagement.

Reflection

This is the project I trace everything else back to. It's where I learned that the most important insight is rarely the one a participant says first — and where I first felt what documentary filmmaking had originally promised me: a story specific enough to one group of people that an institution far outside their experience could still be moved to act on it.
