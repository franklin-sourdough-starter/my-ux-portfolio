Overview

What I did

I led the end-to-end design of the onboarding and transition experience for Kroger's new enterprise item-management platform. Working across eight distinct user groups, I ran discovery interviews and contextual inquiry, synthesized the findings into per-group problem definitions, mapped the support structure the transition would need, validated it directly with users, then shipped a three-layer framework — a main guide, role-specific guides, and a role-aware AI assistant.

Context

Item data is the backbone of Kroger

Every item's data — its attributes, pricing, packaging, distribution rules — determines everything downstream: what shows up at a distribution center, how it moves through the supply chain, and what a customer finds on the shelf at a Kroger store. Get that data wrong, or too slow, and the effects ripple across the entire business.

But the way that data actually gets managed today is fragmented: multiple legacy systems and countless spreadsheets, stitched together as much by institutional memory as by process. Kroger's modernization effort aims to retire those legacy systems and Excel-centered workflows in favor of a single, centralized item-management platform.

My project was to design the bridge between the two — the onboarding and transition experience that would carry each role-specific user group from the old way of working into the new one, without breaking the flow of a business that runs on this data every day.

Process

A Double Diamond, run twice

I structured the project as a Double Diamond: one diamond to understand the problem, one to build the solution. Discover and Define converged on a clear picture of who was affected and how they actually worked today. Develop and Deliver diverged again to design the support structure, validate it with real users, then ship and iterate.

DiscoverTalk to everyone touching the legacy system.
DefineTurn raw interviews into a problem per group.
DevelopDesign the support structure, then validate it.
DeliverShip the layers, test with users, iterate.
Phase 1 — Discover

Understanding a process that barely existed

I reached out to SMEs across more than 20 different roles, running interviews and contextual inquiry to understand two things at once: what the current onboarding process looked like for each group — often informally, or not at all — and how they actually worked inside the legacy systems day to day, not just how the documentation said they should.

Phase 2 — Define

Turning 20+ conversations into a problem per group

I used Dovetail and affinity mapping to clean up the raw interview data and organize it by user group, then gathered the documentation each group actually referenced to do their jobs in the legacy system today. The end of Define was a clear problem definition and a condensed set of pain points for every group — not a single generic list, since each group's version of the problem looked different.

For each group, I mapped their Jobs To Be Done — the recurring tasks, frustrations, and underlying goals any onboarding support would need to address. Below is the board for one of the eight groups, FC Specialist: understanding how the new platform differs from the legacy tool, reviewing and editing item data, navigating tasks efficiently, publishing data, task-assignment inefficiency, handling errors, decision-tree dependency, and lack of visibility after task completion.

Phase 3 — Develop

Mapping the transition before designing the guide

I diverged again to develop a high-level structure for the onboarding support itself. Two artifacts drove this: a communication-channel diagram, mapping every touchpoint a user has during onboarding, and an experience workflow blueprint, laying out the transition phase by phase — pre-launch, launch day, and post-launch — with the touchpoint, user action, and support action at each step.

Once I had a directional structure, I scheduled sessions with each user group to run user-acceptance testing: validating that I'd understood their legacy workflow correctly, and checking whether the proposed support structure actually matched what they needed.

Phase 4 — Deliver

Shipping the three-layer framework, then iterating

Once the structure was validated, I fleshed it out into the full three-layer learning support framework:

  • A main guide — the shared mental model every role needs, showing where the platform adds value against the old, error-prone way of working.
  • Role-specific guides — tailored walkthroughs for each user group, so a merchandiser and a supply-chain analyst aren't taught the platform through the same lens.
  • A role-aware AI onboarding assistant — an in-platform agent I configured with custom prompts and knowledge context, so users could ask plain-language questions about their own workflows instead of hunting through documentation.

I had users test directly with the guide and the chatbot, then iterated the content and the assistant's responses based on their feedback.

I presented the vision and the implementation roadmap to more than 50 cross-functional stakeholders across product, engineering, and business — and the framework itself is now the baseline the team will use to onboard the remaining four user groups by the end of the fiscal year.

Reflection

This project is the clearest version of my journey I can point to. The same instinct that made me want to sit with people until I understood their situation — from anthropology — showed up as contextual inquiry and JTBD synthesis across 20+ SMEs. And the instinct to make something an audience could feel — from filmmaking — showed up in a guide and an assistant designed around each role's actual moment of confusion, not a generic tutorial. The Double Diamond just gave that instinct a shape I could repeat, group by group.
