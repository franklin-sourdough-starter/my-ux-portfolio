Designing the Onboarding Layer for Kroger's Enterprise Item Platform

A three-layer learning framework and a role-aware AI onboarding assistant, built to carry ten user groups through a migration off legacy systems and into a mission-critical merchandising and supply-chain tool.

Role
Product Design Intern
Organization
The Kroger Co.
Duration
May 2026 – Present
Methods
Journey mapping, contextual inquiry, interviews, focus groups
Context

A system too important to learn by trial and error

Kroger's item-management platform sits at the center of merchandising and supply-chain operations — the system of record that ten different role-specific teams touch every day to keep items, pricing, and inventory data correct across the enterprise. As the company began migrating users off the legacy tools this platform replaces, there was no structured way to teach ten very different roles how to actually work inside it.

Getting onboarding wrong here isn't a minor friction cost. A confused edit to item data can ripple into pricing, inventory, and fulfillment across the business. My job was to design the layer that stands between "we migrated the system" and "people can actually trust themselves to use it."

My Role & Approach

Research first, then design in three layers

I led the onboarding strategy end to end, working alongside 4 senior product designers and 5 product managers. Before designing anything, I ran mixed-method research — journey mapping, contextual inquiry, interviews, and focus groups — with more than 10 user groups to understand where their workflows actually broke down, role by role.

Out of that research, I designed a three-layer learning support framework rather than a single generic tutorial:

  • A main guide — the shared mental model every role needs, showing where the platform adds value against the old, error-prone way of working.
  • Role-specific guides — tailored walkthroughs for each of the 10 user groups, so a merchandiser and a supply-chain analyst aren't taught the platform through the same lens.
  • A role-aware AI onboarding assistant — an in-platform agent I configured with custom prompts and knowledge context, so users could ask plain-language questions about their own workflows instead of hunting through documentation.
The AI Layer

Teaching an assistant to onboard, not just answer

The AI assistant was the piece I owned most directly. Rather than a generic chatbot bolted onto the platform, I wrote the system prompt, structured the knowledge context, and built reusable "skills" so the assistant could give role-aware guidance — recognizing whether someone was transitioning from the legacy system or brand new to the platform, and adjusting its answers accordingly.

The goal was to reduce how often a confused user had to interrupt a teammate or file a support ticket just to complete a routine task, especially during the highest-friction weeks of the legacy migration.

Alignment

Designing the story, not just the flow

A framework like this only works if the org actually adopts it. I presented the onboarding vision and implementation roadmap to more than 50 cross-functional stakeholders across product, engineering, and business — the same instinct from documentary filmmaking showing up again: findings and frameworks only matter if you can make the room feel why they matter.

Reflection

This project is the clearest version of my journey I can point to: the same instinct that made me want to sit with people until I understood their situation — from anthropology — showed up as contextual inquiry with ten different roles. And the instinct to make something an audience could feel — from filmmaking — showed up as a guide and an assistant designed around each role's actual moment of confusion, not a generic tutorial.
