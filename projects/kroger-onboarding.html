Research

Modernizing a Legacy System: Designing for the Onboarding Experience to Kroger's Enterprise Item Management Platform

Item data is the backbone of Kroger's business. I ran a Double Diamond process, from cross-functional alignment through SME interviews to a shipped three-layer support framework. The result: an onboarding and transition experience that carries teams off fragmented legacy systems and spreadsheets and onto a single, centralized item-management platform.

Overview

What I did

I led the end-to-end design of the onboarding and transition experience for Kroger's new enterprise item-management platform. Working across four distinct user groups, I aligned cross-functionally on timeline and dependencies, ran discovery research, synthesized findings into per-group problem definitions, mapped and validated a support structure, then created a three-layer framework (a main guide, role-specific guides, and a role-aware AI assistant) along with the maintenance structure for the internal team to keep it alive.

Context

Item data is the backbone of Kroger

Every item's data (its attributes, pricing, packaging, distribution rules) determines everything downstream: what shows up at a distribution center, how it moves through the supply chain, and what a customer finds on the shelf at a Kroger store. Get that data wrong, or too slow, and the effects ripple across the entire business.

But the way that data actually gets managed today is fragmented: multiple legacy systems and countless spreadsheets, stitched together as much by institutional memory as by process. Kroger's modernization effort aims to retire those legacy systems and Excel-centered workflows, manual and fragmented, in favor of a single, centralized item-management platform that's unified and intuitive.

My project was to design the bridge between the two: the onboarding and transition experience that would carry each role-specific user group from the old way of working into the new one, without breaking the flow of a business that runs on this data every day.

Cross-Functional Alignment

Before the Double Diamond: aligning on requirement and timeline

A project like this doesn't start with research. It starts with alignment. Before I ran any interviews, I worked with 5 product managers and 3 product designers, across 2 separate teams (task management and edit experience) to align on the onboarding timeline and the dependencies between our workstreams. Onboarding couldn't be designed in a vacuum: it had to land in sync with each team's own launch dates, feature freezes, and cutover plans.

That meant constant, ongoing communication, not a single kickoff meeting. I stayed in the loop on priority shifts and shipped-date changes from both teams so the onboarding plan never fell out of sync with what was actually being built.

Process

A Double Diamond

With the timeline aligned, I followed the Double Diamond process in the research and design work: one diamond to understand the problem, one to build the solution. Emerging out of the Discover and Define phases, I converged on a clear picture of who was affected and how they actually worked today. I then diverged again to design the support structure, validate it with real users, then converged to ship and iterate.

DiscoverTalk to SMEs, users, and everyone that is familiar with the legacy system.
DefineTurn raw interviews into problem statements and actionable insights.
DevelopDesign the support structure, then validate it.
DeliverTest the support structure with users. Then iterate.
Phase 1: Discover

Understanding a process that barely existed

I reached out to users and SMEs across 19 different roles, running interviews, focus groups, and contextual inquiry to understand two things at once: what the current onboarding process looked like for each group, often informally or not at all, and how they actually worked inside the legacy systems day to day, not just how the documentation said they should.

Who I talked to

The 19 SMEs I interviewed spanned data integrity, retail operations, e-commerce fulfillment, and digital/product teams: the full width of people whose work touches item data before it ever reaches a shelf.

Phase 2: Define

Turning 19 conversations into a problem per group

I used Dovetail and affinity mapping to clean up the raw interview data and organize it by user group, then gathered the documentation each group actually referenced to do their jobs in the legacy system today. The end of Define was a clear problem definition and a condensed set of pain points for every group, not a single generic list, since each group's version of the problem looked different.

Jobs to be done

For each group, I mapped their Jobs To Be Done: the recurring workflows, frustrations, and underlying goals any onboarding support would need to address. Below is the board for one of the eight groups, FC (Fulfillment Center) Specialist: understanding how the new platform differs from the legacy tool, reviewing and editing item data, navigating tasks efficiently, publishing data, task-assignment inefficiency, handling errors, decision-tree dependency, and lack of visibility after task completion.

Contextual inquiry per user group

Alongside JTBD, I contributed to a contextual inquiry board for each user group: a standard template capturing their work environment and primary responsibilities, working tasks, primary views into the legacy system, and the artifacts (spreadsheets, reference docs) they relied on day to day.

Phase 3: Develop

Mapping the transition before designing the guide

I diverged again to develop a high-level structure for the onboarding support itself. Two artifacts drove this: a communication-channel diagram, mapping every touchpoint a user has during onboarding, and an experience workflow blueprint, laying out the transition phase by phase (pre-launch, launch day, and post-launch) with the touchpoint, user action, and support action mapped in separate swimlanes at each step.

Once I had a directional structure, I scheduled sessions with each user group to run user-acceptance testing: validating that I'd understood their legacy workflow correctly, and checking whether the proposed support structure actually matched what they needed.

Phase 4: Deliver

Creating the three-layer structure, then validating and iterating

Once the onboarding plan was validated by users, I fleshed it out into the full three-layer learning support structure, each layer answering a different question a user actually has at a different moment in their onboarding.

Layer 1: The Overview Guide

The main guide is a comprehensive 60-page handbook built for both current legacy-tool users and brand-new hires, and it's structured around three questions: What is the new platform? How has the experience changed? What are the best practices for working efficiently in it? It covers the core capabilities, navigations, and workflows every role needs before anything role-specific.

I designed the guide to be interactive, not a static PDF: a clickable table of contents for jumping straight to the section you need, dark mode for readability, and inline links out to the reference documents each topic is drawn from.

Layer 2: Role-Specific Guides

The main guide gives everyone the same mental model; the role-specific guides answer a narrower, more personal question: If I were a new hire today, how would I actually perform my job in the new system without asking someone else, and how do I do my job most efficiently? Each of these role-specific guides walks through that role's real workflows, not a generic tour of the platform.

Layer 3: The Custom AI Assistant

Guides solve the "I need to learn this" problem. They don't solve the "I need an answer right now" problem. Users onboarding onto a new system are still busy doing their jobs; they don't want to search through a document to find one field's validation rule. The assistant exists to answer the question What's the fastest way to get help?, giving on-demand guidance, troubleshooting, and direct access to reference documentation, without anyone having to leave their workflow.

Choosing the platform: Microsoft Copilot Studio vs. a custom-built agent by Kroger

I first explored building the assistant in Microsoft Copilot Studio. In testing, I ran into blocking issues, including a knowledge source blocked by data policy and conflicting data-policy groups preventing publication, that made it a real feasibility and data-security risk for handling internal item data.

I opted instead for Kroger's own internal custom-agent platform. It sidestepped the data-policy conflicts, kept everything inside Kroger's existing security boundary, and was the more cost-effective path to a production assistant.

Designing the system prompt

The assistant is only as good as the behavior I write into it. I structured the system prompt around context, users, and core capabilities (onboarding support, workflow guidance, reference support, troubleshooting), then went deep on conversation flow, tone, and, critically, where the assistant should stop.

Tone and capability. The output guidelines define the assistant's voice and style directly: how formal to be, how to phrase corrections, how to stay consistent across a long conversation. It also has a dedicated cross-cultural sensitivity section, since the assistant supports a workforce that isn't culturally uniform.

Off-ramps. I designed explicit rules for when the assistant should redirect rather than answer: out-of-scope Kroger systems, features not documented in the training guide, bug reports, and access disputes each get their own acknowledge → redirect → engage pattern, with example phrasing so the tone stays consistent even when the assistant is saying "I can't help with that."

Image handling. Users can upload screenshots of the platform to ask questions, so I wrote explicit rules for how the assistant should interpret them: describe what it sees factually, map it to the relevant guide, read validation states from visual cues like field color and icon, and never guess at content it can't clearly read.

I had users test directly with the guide and the chatbot, then iterated the content and the assistant's responses based on their feedback. I also presented the vision and the implementation roadmap to more than 50 cross-functional stakeholders across product, engineering, and business. The framework itself is now the baseline the team will use to onboard the remaining four user groups by the end of the fiscal year.

Post-Launch

A living project, not a one-time deliverable

An onboarding framework that only I can update isn't actually done. Before the internship ended, I built the maintenance structure so the internal team can keep extending it, to the remaining four user groups and beyond, without needing me in the room.

I documented the assistant itself on Confluence: what it does, how to access it, and how to maintain it through Kroger's internal tools, so any team member can pick up ownership.

Keeping the assistant's knowledge base current means the team needs a repeatable way to add new reference material. I documented a naming convention so every context document is traceable to the user group and topic it covers, and paired it with a custom Claude Code skill that converts source documents (Excel, PDF, Word, PowerPoint, images) into the clean Markdown format the assistant's knowledge base actually needs.

This structure is what makes the "4 of 8 user groups" number a starting point rather than a ceiling: the team has the process, the naming system, and the tooling to keep going.

Reflection

Looking back, a few things stand out about how I approached this project. I spent real time with people until I understood their situation, which here meant contextual inquiry and JTBD synthesis with 19 SMEs, plus a lot of cross-functional alignment with PMs and designers. But I never wanted to stop at the insight. The goal was to make people's first weeks on the new platform actually easier, not just to write up where they were struggling. That's also why I built the maintenance structure before I left: the work only matters if it keeps working once I'm not around to maintain it.
